The Industrial Revolution began in Britain during the late eighteenth century. Steam power transformed manufacturing across the textile industry. Iron production scaled dramatically as new smelting techniques emerged. Cities expanded rapidly as workers migrated from rural areas. Coal mining became essential to fuel the new factories. Canal networks were built to move goods between industrial centers. The first commercial steam railway opened in 1825 between Stockton and Darlington. Wages for skilled workers improved over the long term despite periodic downturns. The middle class grew substantially in industrial cities. Public health conditions in factory towns initially declined sharply. Reform movements pressed for safer working conditions throughout the nineteenth century. Trade unions emerged to negotiate on behalf of workers. Education laws expanded literacy among working-class children. Steam engines were eventually replaced by electric motors in many applications. Telegraph networks revolutionized long-distance communication. Photography began with the daguerreotype process in the 1830s. The British Empire reached its territorial peak around 1920. Naval steam power changed the strategic balance of major powers. The Suez Canal opened in 1869, shortening trade routes substantially. International expositions showcased new technologies to vast audiences. The 1851 Great Exhibition in London drew six million visitors. Public museums and libraries spread to most major cities. Metropolitan police forces were established in industrial centers. Sanitation reforms reduced disease outbreaks in urban areas. The discovery of germ theory transformed medical practice. Anaesthesia made complex surgery routinely survivable. Antiseptic techniques reduced post-operative mortality dramatically. Vaccination programs eradicated smallpox in many countries. Compulsory schooling laws reached most industrialized nations by 1900. Universities expanded their student bodies and research programs. Scientific journals proliferated to disseminate new findings. The periodic table was published by Mendeleev in 1869. Electrical theory advanced rapidly through the nineteenth century. Maxwell unified electricity and magnetism in his equations of 1864. Radio waves were first generated experimentally by Hertz in 1887. Marconi sent the first transatlantic radio signal in 1901. Aviation began with the Wright Brothers' powered flight in 1903. Internal combustion engines made automobiles practical for mass markets. Henry Ford introduced moving assembly lines for car production. Electric lighting replaced gas lamps in most cities by 1920. The telephone became widespread in homes during the same period. Cinema emerged as a major art form during the early twentieth century. Modernism in literature broke from nineteenth-century conventions. Cubism rejected traditional perspective in painting. Jazz music originated in New Orleans and spread internationally. Skyscrapers transformed urban skylines beginning in Chicago. Reinforced concrete enabled new architectural possibilities. Bridge engineering reached new spans with cable-stayed designs. Ocean liners reached unprecedented sizes by the early 1900s. The Titanic, completed in 1911, was among the largest ships of its day. International postal services connected most countries by 1900. Time zones were standardized at the 1884 international conference. The metric system spread to most scientific and commercial contexts. The first modern Olympic Games were held in Athens in 1896. Mass-circulation newspapers shaped public opinion in democratic societies. Photography became affordable for ordinary households by the 1900s. Department stores changed urban retail in major cities. Mail-order catalogs brought industrial goods to rural communities. Sewing machines and gas stoves transformed domestic labor. Refrigeration extended food supply chains across continents. Bicycles offered personal mobility to a wide population. Football became professional in England during the 1880s. Public parks were created in most large industrial cities. Working hours fell gradually as productivity rose. Pension systems began to appear in industrialized nations. Insurance against industrial accidents was first legislated in Germany. Universal male suffrage was achieved in most democracies by 1920. Women's suffrage spread internationally from New Zealand outward starting in 1893. Labour parties gained representation in many parliaments. Income tax replaced excise as the main source of state revenue. Public expenditure on health and education rose substantially. Banking systems consolidated under central bank oversight. Bond markets financed long-term infrastructure projects. Stock exchanges in London and New York set global standards. Telegraph cables linked financial centers across the Atlantic. The gold standard provided monetary stability until 1914. International trade reached unprecedented volumes before the First World War. Steel production became a key indicator of national power. Synthetic dyes from coal tar transformed the chemical industry. Petroleum extraction expanded in the United States and Russia. Electric trams replaced horse-drawn carriages in urban transport. Subways were built in London, Paris, and New York. Suburbs grew as commuter rail networks expanded. Public housing schemes addressed overcrowding in working-class areas. Sewage systems became standard infrastructure in major cities. Drinking water supplies were filtered and chlorinated to prevent disease. Mortality rates fell sharply through the late nineteenth century. Life expectancy at birth rose substantially in industrialized nations. Birth rates began to decline as urbanization spread. Family size fell from large agricultural averages toward modern levels. Migration to the Americas accelerated through the 1880s and 1890s. Steamship lines made long-distance migration affordable for working-class families. Cultural diffusion accelerated through cheap printing and global trade. Symphony orchestras became fixtures in capital cities worldwide.
